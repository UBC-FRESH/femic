TASS / TIPSY parameters for aspen

Trembling aspen (Populus tremuloides Michx.) is the second broadleaf hardwood species to be incorporated into both TASS and TIPSY (the first being red alder (Alnus rubra Bong.)).  As with the use of any model where the results may guide important decisions, the user has a responsibility to be aware of the inherent strengths and weaknesses of the support tool.  It is also the responsibility of the developer to make available information to allow for informed decision making.  Due diligence on both sides will ensure an awareness of the levels of inherent risk and confidence associated with model output.  This will allow for reasonable expectations in model output interpretation and in the decision making process.  To this end, a number of issues require the user to be aware of the inherent TASS and TIPSY aspen model limitations (see below). You should be aware that growth and yield information may be either incomplete or missing for specific areas of interest.  Confidence in model results, as always, is directly related to the quality and quantity of available data.

TASS Aspen

  1. Yields for all three regeneration types (planted, natural, clumped) reflect the growth of natural aspen.  The planted option is merely a work-around to enable pro-rating of multiple-species yields involving natural aspen and planted conifers.  However, planted yields will vary slightly from natural yields due to the effect of seedling height on the height/age relationship.

  2. The PSP (permanent sample plot) & EP (experimental plots) datasets used to calibrate TASS for aspen do not exceed site index (SI) 32 m @ BHAge 50 (Nigh et al. 2002).  

  3. The test of “reasonableness" is always recommended in applying model output to operational scenarios.

  4. Calibration efforts focused on fitting TASS tree level growth relationships to primarily British Columbia and Alberta data.  Stand level model calibration was based on approximately 440 PSP data summaries from British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan and Manitoba.  Nigh et al. 2002 site index curves were used during model calibration (the Ministry recommended curves).

  5. During aspen tree level model calibration, TASS crown form was changed to a dynamic crown profile model where changes in shape were dependent upon tree height.  This is unique to aspen crowns and allows model crown development to more closely predict observed changes in tree profiles.  For TASS model behaviour this is important since crown form or shape determines crown volume.  This in turn affects diameter growth and allows spatial interaction between trees, crown competition or tree occupancy.  

  6. Aspen is a relatively short lived species. Temporary sample plot (TSP) data from BC and Alberta suggest that aspen stand age rarely exceeds 160 years, though older stand and tree ages are possible.  For the purposes of TASS stand level calibration, however, we significantly increased stand mortality or induced stand break-up at ages >120 years.  To simulate age related stand break-up, TASS random mortality was increased after stand age 140 years, resulting in complete stand mortality by 160 years.  We have defined this as “age decline”.

  7. TASS defines top height as the average height of the 100 largest diameter trees per hectare.  It can be shown that, statistically, top height of a given stand will increase with increasing initial density.  This is sometimes referred to as “genetic upgrade”.  Within normal managed stand densities the increase in top height is small.  However, the difference between a stand density of for example, 20,000 sph and one with 2,000 sph can result in a significant top height and concurrent volume increase.  This affect is difficult to document without rigorous long term field research.  Increases in top height with density have not been documented in aspen.  In response to this observation, a height vigour adjustment (HVA) was developed which forces TASS top height trees to follow the site index curve regardless of initial density. This simplifying convenience allows TASS growth and yield information to be easily interpolated across all top heights in the TIPSY database.  For TASS runs, it is important to specify whether the HVA is turned ON or OFF and to understand the implications of the choice.

  8. Aspen PSP data from young stands where all trees were measured for height and DBH indicated a wider DBH and height distribution than expected. The data suggested that aspen height growth variation is significantly larger than that found in conifer species such as lodgepole pine and Douglas-fir.  Twenty-one TASS equation coefficients (Mitchell 1975) are used to set and control the initial tree population variation through the assignment of individual tree height growth vigour coefficients (HVC).  One of the underlying assumptions of TASS is the requirement of a normal distribution of HVC’s with a relatively narrow tree to tree variation.  A narrow variation is necessary to ensure that crowns compete.

    Analysis of aspen PSP data from various sources (BC and Manitoba) indicated the average aspen stand has a height growth distribution that is not well represented by a normal distribution.  On first approximation the Poisson distribution may more accurately predict the height growth frequency of an average aspen stand.  This divergence from the normal distribution may be related to height growth suppression at the relatively high initial densities found in most aspen stands.  For the present, TASS aspen HVC’s are determined using the normal distribution (t- distribution) with no allowance for a significantly wider variation than previously seen (m aspen=0.4799, compared to m lodgepole pine=0.8).

    TASS aspen tends to consistently underestimate the average DBH by 1-2 cm at maturity (in comparison with PSP data).  This might be a result of a number of contributing factors such as poor fit with observed field DBH and height distributions, and unresponsive tree DBH growth at very young ages.   At present, these issues appear to be irresolvable.

  9. TASS / TIPSY volume over age curves are comparable to known PSP datasets across the range of SI (site index).  TASS simulates mortality for medium to high sites well but is less accurate as site quality declines.   Aspen PSP data suggests that TASS progressively overestimates density and underestimates DBH as site class declines and stands age.  Also, observed aspen density does not follow the model assumption of similar densities at similar top heights across sites.  For example, at stand age 60 on SI 12, TASS density may be 50% higher than the PSP data shows, with a concomitant reduction in DBH of up to 50%. At stand age 120, TASS density may be up to 3 times that in the PSP data.  This model behaviour should be considered for all aspen sites where SI ≤ 15 m.  

  10. Aspen stand regeneration relies heavily on the initiation and growth of root suckers.  Although aspen will regenerate from seed, the primary source of new stems is from root suckers which initiate and grow after a disturbance removes or kills the aboveground stem.  The review paper by Frey et al. (2003) provides a summary of the available knowledge on aspen sucker regeneration.  It has been found that the initiation and growth of young aspen stands is dependent upon a number of physiological, environmental and disturbance regime factors.  Events that impact the distribution and abundance of healthy aspen roots after harvesting may also impact stand regeneration success (initial sucker density, spatial distribution and growth) (Frey et al. 2003).  For the TIPSY user, it is important to be aware that TASS model calibration was based upon data from natural stands where root sucker regeneration occurred primarily after disturbance by fire.  As a result, the model doesn't account for impacts on aspen stand regeneration that are related to harvesting.  

  11. Simulations of young, low density aspen stands (< ~2500 sph??) are outside the range of available data.  Information suggests that, at low densities where crown closure is not reached early, these stands may suffer from poor growth and higher mortality due to vegetation competition from grass such as Calamagrostis canadensis and shrub species.  A number of studies have indicated the need for high aspen densities to facilitate rapid crown closure and the shading of competing grasses and shrubs (Frey et al. 2003).

TIPSY Aspen

  1. TIPSY pre-commercial thinning (PCT) simulations are available only at top height 4.0m, which is consistent with other interior species. TASS projections should be requested for greater flexibility in PCT and CT simulations.  Pruning is only available through TASS.  

  2. The TIPSY database does not simulate planted aspen.  The spatial distributions available are random and clumped only.  The planting of aspen is usually only attempted when reclaiming cleared farm land or establishing research installations (for example, genetic provenance trials).  Special requests can be made for TASS runs of aspen plantations.

  3. TIPSY allows the user to create a mixed species stand composition for all conifer species.  This option is not allowed with aspen and red alder because they are short-lived species and both TASS and TIPSY are unable to simulate species interaction as a stand breaks-up.  

  4. TIPSY does not provide aspen lumber recovery information.  Aspen logs are manufactured into OSB (oriented strand board), LVL (laminated veneer lumber), LSL (laminated strand lumber), and plywood as well as into pallet and container lumber.  In recent years, there has been interest in the primary manufacture of aspen lumber into 1” and 2” material for the European and Japanese export market.  Hardwood log and lumber grading rules are extremely complex (USFS hardwood log grading rules and National Hardwood Lumber Association (NHLA) hardwood lumber grades).

  5. Accurate log values and lumber recovery statistics for aspen are presently unavailable.  As a result, please be aware that FAN$IER default economic values are based on softwoods.

  6. To facilitate TIPSY database interpolation routines TASS runs were configured with a specific set of parameters.  The parameters are:

    • HVA turned ON

    • age decline turned OFF, and

    • regeneration delay set to zero.

  1. TIPSY routines adjust for age decline and regeneration delay.  As a result, TASS and TIPSY output may be similar but not identical.

  2. In TIPSY an Age Decline Custom OAF is enabled as the default.  The user can easily turn it off and create a new Custom OAF to replace it.
