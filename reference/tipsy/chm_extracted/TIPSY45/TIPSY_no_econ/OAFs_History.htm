Early OAFs pre-TIPSY (1985 – 1991)

TASS was calibrated to match data from remeasured research plots located in fully-stocked stands (Mitchell and Cameron 1985a). Before using these yield projections in timber supply analyses, they were reduced to conform to conditions in the operational environment. When this work began, small non-productive areas (NP, e.g. rock outcrops, brush pockets) were of particular concern. Since local information was inadequate, researchers consulted with other agencies and the literature when developing yield projection systems for coastal Douglas-fir in the mid 80’s. The US Forest Service routinely reduced their yields to 90% of the yield generated by their models unless better local information was available (personal communication). A formal study in Finland reported operational volumes of 88% and 80% for pine and spruce stands, respectively, in the vicinity of research plots (Oikarinen 1978). A Swedish study recommended 85% based on a study that reported 85% standing volume, and 90% for volume growth over 39 years (Eriksson 1967). Inventory samples from unmanaged stands in BC produced about 65 to 85% of the volume found in research plots. This information led to the decision to adopt 85% for operational yields in managed forests in BC.   It was applied to inventories with a minimum polygon size of approximately 2ha, i.e., the smallest NP mapped.

Subsequent work added a single Decay Soundwood losses due to physical and chemical processes caused by wood-rotting fungi., Waste Soundwood portion of a tree or log that has more than 50% decay. Considered to be non-recoverable under current utilization standards. and Breakage Soundwood losses due to felling and yarding breakage; not merchantable because the broken pieces are considered too small (DWB) factor to the OAF.  Inventory studies indicated DWB reduced the yield of second-growth stands to 96% of the cruise volume.  Consequently, a standard OAF of 0.816 (i.e. 0.85 x 0.96) was applied to all volume estimates generated by TASS (Mitchell and Cameron 1985b). More precise local information was rarely available or applied at that time. Little effort had been made to identify the agents that contributed to this reduction, or the myriad of other factors that can affect operational yields, particularly as stands approach maturity.  Consequently, TASS yield curves were "pulled down" a little beyond a height of 40m to account for these other factors.   However, this was eventually eliminated with the introduction of OAF2, and concurrent recalibration of TASS in the early 90’s.

OAF Evolution (1991 - present)

This single OAF developed for TASS was divided into OAF1 and OAF2 when TIPSY was introduced in 1991, and used in both systems thereafter. OAF1 (default of 0.85) accounted for non-productive (NP) areas, plus minor impacts from various biotic factors. The new OAF2 featured an increasing percentage reduction over time. It approximated DWB in second-growth stands, but did not include growth losses caused by insects, diseases and other pests. OAF2 can, however, be used to accommodate pests if specialists can quantify the impact in terms of OAF2, or OAF1. The default value for OAF2 was set at 0.95 at age 100, resulting in a value closer to 0.96 (above) at a typical harvest age.   Previous versions of TIPSY also included a separate DWB OAF that was never adopted operationally.  It was eventually dropped from TIPSY due to technical concerns.

In the late 1990's, several ministry and industry projects emerged to estimate OAF1 with field sampling focussed on stocking gaps.   In response, Nussbaum (2001) partitioned OAF1 into four factors (below) to highlight the presence of other factors besides non-productive (NP) ground within the standard ministry default of 0.85.  Arbitrary (~equal) percentages were proposed to encourage discussion.

  1. Non-productive (NP) ‘holes’ within the stand that are too small to map [delineate] in a conventional inventory (~4% reduction).

  2. Holes in a stand caused by irregular stand establishment, competition from non-commercial brush and elements that cause trees to be more irregularly spaced then is assumed in TASS and TIPSY (~4% reduction).

  3. Endemic disease and insect losses over the life of the stand such as spruce weevil, rusts and needle casts (~4% reduction).

  4. Other random risk factors such as windthrow, top damage and snow press (~3% reduction).

Subsequently, OAF1 estimates from field sampling projects are usually tempered to acknowledge unquantified factors, before they are accepted in timber supply analyses.
