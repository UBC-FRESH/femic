OAFs: Example Applications

The application of OAFs requires both experience and judgment, as illustrated with the following hypothetical cases.  Note, with better information, Custom OAFs might be a better choice in cases 2 to 5.

Case 1 (many small openings: crown occupation)

You’ve planted trees 2.5 m apart over a large area. Theoretically, you should be able to plant 1600 trees/ha. However, past experience or an entomologist tells you that the root collar weevil will kill about 40% of the trees before the crowns close. You also know that mortality is likely to be randomly distributed throughout the plantation. Since we are concerned about many small dispersed holes rather than a few large ones, it would be better to have TIPSY plant 960 (1600 - 640) trees/ha and ignore OAF1. In doing so, we assume the holes are small and uniformly distributed. This is a little optimistic because TIPSY will assume the crowns close faster than in the operational world. The volume impact of reducing the number of trees from 1600 to 960 is small.

To specify 1600 trees/ha and an OAF1 of 40% is far too pessimistic because it assumes that 40% of the site is out of production for the life of the stand as is illustrated in Case 2.

Case 2 (few large openings: no crown occupation)

Now assume you observe, on average, one hole per hectare that reduces the available growing space by 40%. Here it’s appropriate to tell TIPSY that you intended to plant 1600 trees/ha (prescribed spacing of 2.5 m) but fell short by 40% due to rock outcrops. Simply set the plantation establishment density to 1600 and OAF 1 to 0.60. Notice the warning about prime tree statistics. The impact on yield is severe in this rather extreme example.

Case 3 (moderate openings: limited crown occupation)

Now imagine a forest in which 40% of the area is covered by many brush pockets of moderate size. However, you believe that the surrounding crowns will expand into these openings and benefit from the extra growing space. This situation can be addressed by an OAF2 greater than 1.00. Simply leave OAF1 set to 0.60, as in Case 2, and supplement it with an OAF2 of 1.10. It elevates the volume in the later years relative to the previous curve.

NOTE:  While individual OAFs may be greater than 1.00, but they cannot be combined to increase yield above 1.00.   Therefore, if the net OAF exceeds 1.00 at any age, TIPSY will ‘cap’ it at 1.00 for that particular age.   A message appears when that occurs.

Case 4 (pest impact: crown occupation)

Assume the growth of your stands is impaired by pests, or possibly snow press, sometime after crown closure. Many holes are created which decrease in size as the crowns of neighbouring trees invade the vacant growing space. The growth rate (MAI) increases and achieves that of the unaffected stand by age 135, although stand volume is still considerably less. This example is basically the same as Case 3. Try changing OAF2 to 1.20 in the preceding example and you’ll likely find that the volume is down by about 25% by age 135, but the growth rates (MAI) are almost identical.

Case 5 (expanding pockets of root rot)

Now for a very common application of OAFs. You have a plantation of coastal Douglas-fir in which patches of root rot appear about age 50 and increase in size for the remainder of the rotation. Set OAF1 to 1.10 and OAF2 to 0.80 and compare the volume with an uninfected plantation. You’ll see that the curves are almost identical until age 50, but diverge rapidly thereafter. You can adjust the OAFs to achieve a reduction in standing volume if the stand is held beyond 110 years. Then combine an OAF1 of 1.25 and an OAF2 of 0.60. It’s up to you and your pathologist to decide if the impact approximates the conditions expected in the field.
