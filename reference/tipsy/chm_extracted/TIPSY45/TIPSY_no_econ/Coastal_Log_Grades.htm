Introduction

The Ministry of Forests Revenue Branch (Ministry of Forests, 1997) uses log transactions on the Vancouver log market to derive timber end-product values in the coastal region of B.C. We estimate average market values by species and log grade using these transactions. Log grades reflect the quality of the timber contained within a log, and the prices reflect the value of the products that can be produced from timber of this quality. Table 3-3 lists the current letter grades adopted in 1990 for grading timber on the coast, and some required specifications for logs in each grade. See Ministry of Forests 1995 for more details of the coastal log grading system. Note that not all grades are used for all species. The table excludes yellow cedar.

TIPSY's Log Grading Limitations

Since we are limited in our current capabilities to model timber quality, we had to simplify the log grades and grading rules used to produce the simulated log product table. Douglas-fir peeler grades B and C, D and F grades for all species, spruce grades E and G and the cedar shingle grades K, L and M were dropped. Thus, a common grade set of H, I, J, U, X, and Y was adopted for all species.

Note that, in order to meet these grade requirements, the D and F grade logs must produce 50% and 25% of clear lumber respectively. The relatively poor self-pruning characteristics of our coniferous species suggest that our second-growth stands will produce no clear lumber within a reasonable rotation period unless pruning is undertaken. As TIPSY doesn't yet include the effects of pruning, no D or F grade logs can be produced.

The H grade requirement of a minimum 5 annual growth rings per 2 cm currently creates problems within TIPSY. TIPSY can't translate this information across the range of site indexes and thus the distribution of volume between grades H and I may be incorrect. The distribution will be correct for site index 35 on the coast. In the interior, it will be correct for lodgepole pine and white spruce at site index 20, and douglas-fir at site index 25. The H grade will be underestimated at lower site indexes and overestimated at higher site indexes. Note that for cedar there is no error in the grades as the minimum growth rings per cm doesn't apply to cedar.

While future versions of TIPSY may attempt to address this potential grade bias, there is reason to believe that it will not be a serious problem, especially when we consider that the stands won't be harvested for several decades. The current log grading rules were developed to handle the mainly old-growth harvest on the coast. As we move into the harvest of second-growth logs, we may very well see a loosening of some of these grading requirements. For example, in the US PNW where they now harvest mainly second-growth timber, their grading rules have no rings per inch requirements for their No. 2 sawmill log grades for Douglas-fir, Sitka spruce or western white pine (Puget Sound Log Scaling & Grading Bureau, 1996). Their No. 2 sawmill grading requirements are similar to B.C.'s H grades for these species. In addition, the US PNW system has no ring width requirements for western hemlock grades. If the TIPSY database used similar rules, it would eliminate all of the potential grading bias.

Log Grade Distribution

The percentage of total standing volume in each log grade will of course change over time. As a stand ages, all other factors held constant, the increasing size of the boles produces logs with higher grades. Thus, timber shifts to higher and higher grades with age. This is shown in Figure 3-10 below. In Panel A of the figure, we see that by age 20 some of the trees in the stand are large enough to produce merchantable logs. Naturally these logs are mainly in the U and J grades. As the stand ages, more and more trees meet the minimum merchantable dimensions, but continue to yield mostly J and U grade logs. At approximately age 45, the stand starts to yield I grade logs while H grade logs don't appear until age 65. Panel B shows the percentage share of total merchantable volume in each log grade over time. The share of total merchantable volume in the J grade increases sharply from age 20, reaches a maximum at approximately age 45, and then declines as the logs shift upward into the I and H grades.

Figure 3-11 below shows how initial density affects the grade distribution for a stand of western hemlock site index 30 with no OAFs. It plots the volumes of H, I and J grade logs produced by a natural stand and a planted stand. First, note that there is little difference between the stands in the volume of H grade logs produced. This is because the H grade logs would come from the dominant trees with crowns extending above the general level of the canopy and receiving full light from above and partly from the side; taller than the average trees in the stand with crowns well developed trees in both stands, and there is little difference between the size of the dominant trees in these stands (Mitchell and Goudie, 1999). The dominants in both stands have escaped the competition from surrounding trees and are only slightly affected by initial density. Second, note that the lower initial density stand produces J and I grade logs at an earlier age than the higher density stand. Initial density has its greatest biological impact on the development of lower canopy trees, so it's not surprising that higher densities retard the "economic" development of these trees.

Density affects log grades not only through piece size but also through knot size, taper and wood density (as measured by rings per cm). If initial densities are too low, the negative wood quality aspects of the lower density could completely offset the positive aspect of larger log sizes. See Jozsa and Middleton (1994) for a more complete discussion of wood quality attributes.

Naturally, the stand's site index also affects the grade distribution, as the slower growing sites move logs up the grades at a slower rate. At the other extreme, however, if a tree grows too quickly, the logs cut from the bole can be degraded since they won't meet the minimum rings per cm requirements of the grading rules.
