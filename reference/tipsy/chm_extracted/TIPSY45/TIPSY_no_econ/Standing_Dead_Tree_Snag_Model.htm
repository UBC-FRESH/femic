Model Description

The snag (standing dead tree) model simply determines the number of trees that are still standing, from those that have died since stand establishment. A dead tree is considered standing if it is greater than 1.3 m in height and self supported. We chose a logistic model to describe the binary data (i.e., standing or fallen) associated with the fall of standing dead trees. The logistic model is preferable because predictions are limited between 0 and 1, and linearizing the model via a logit transformation (i.e., log(p/1-p)) allows the use of linear regression methods.

The standing dead tree model is limited to the two independent variables, years since death and DBH, that are known to be related to the fall rate. Other variables which may be important are cause of death, wind conditions, and density of surrounding standing dead trees. These variables are excluded, however, because of either the lack of data, or because TIPSY isn't able to generate this data.

Application in TIPSY

To generate a Snag Table, mortality tables in the TIPSY database are first transformed from their original height steps to age steps. The Snag Model is applied directly to these age-based mortality tables, and then interpolated.

This sequence of model application and interpolation results in consistent estimates between model runs for identical stands, but of different age steps (e.g., between 1 year and 10 year steps). You would, however, notice slight differences if you compared the Snag Table to a manual calculation of the model applied directly to the data in the interpolated Mortality Table.

NOTE:  To apply the OAF2 Operational Adjustment Factor, TIPSY's programmers had to adjust the mortality table to account for increasing mortality. To do this, they applied OAF2 and then compared the changes in the live tree stand tables to the non-adjusted mortality tables. But they weren't able to determine the exact diameter class of the trees that had died at each age step. As a result, the adjustments are slightly biased (e.g., mean dead tree height is biased upwards). Considering the large variation already inherent within the statistics, you should view these biases as insignificant.

Model Equations

Specifically, the deterministic component of the model can be described as:

ln (probfallen/1-probfallen) = b0 + b1*(YearsDead) + b2*(DBH) + b3*(YearsDead*DBH)

or

probfallen = EXP(b0 + b1*(YearsDead) + b2*(DBH) + b3*(YearsDead*DBH)
1+ EXP(b0 + b1*(YearsDead) + b2*(DBH) + b3*(YearsDead*DBH)

or

probstanding = 1/(1+ EXP(b0 + b1*(YearsDead) + b2*(DBH) + b3*(YearsDead*DBH)))

where ln is the natural logarithm, probfallen is the probability that a standing dead tree has fallen and probstanding is the probability that a standing dead tree is still standing (i.e., 1-probfallen), YearsDead is the number of years since the tree died, DBH is the diameter at breast height, and b0, b1, b2, and b3 are coefficients.

For a description of logistic regression see the Biometrics Information Handbook No. 7 , Cox and Snell (1989), or Hosmer and Lemeshow (1989).

About the Data

The default coefficients for the standing dead tree model are based, where possible, on data from British Columbia. Data on standing dead trees were obtained/collected from existing growth and yield permanent sample plots. We asked these basic questions in model development:

  • Is a dead tree currently standing or fallen?

  • How long has the tree been dead?

  • What was its DBH at death?

Growth and yield records provide information about when the tree died and its DBH. In order to discover whether a tree that died is currently standing or fallen, however, we have to revisit the permanent sample plot. There we can learn whether a tree is standing or fallen, but not when it fell. This results in the data being cohort based (i.e., information on the cohort of trees in the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, ... measurement).

If there was no B.C. data for a species in TIPSY's database, we based estimates for default coefficients on literature or on perceived fall rates relative to other species. Further data are being collected during the remeasurement of permanent sample plots under the growth and yield programs of the Research Branch (contact Louise de Montigny).  As the data become available, they'll be incorporated into the existing (or modified) model.

TIPSY contains some species for which there is no database. These species use site index equations that were developed for that particular species, but TIPSY substitutes a database and many other default values. For example, western larch uses most default values for interior Douglas-fir. As a result of this 'blanket' substitution, some defaults are not based on the best available information. Replace the defaults with localized information whenever possible. The substitutions are displayed at the top of each printed table, or in the Stand Description.

Fdc Notes

Douglas-fir installations of EP703 provided the information on Douglas-fir fall rates. EP703 is a large thinning and fertilization experiment in pure Douglas-fir, pure western hemlock, and mixed Douglas-fir/western hemlock stands covering a range of site conditions. This experiment has over 940 plots (0.05 to 0.10 ha in size) located on 85 installations. The installations were established from 1971-1974 and measured every 3 years for the first 12 years, and at least every 6 years thereafter. See Darling and Omule (1989) and Stone (1994) for more details.

The data set for Douglas-fir in TIPSY was limited to the control plots of pure (>80% by basal area) Douglas-fir stands which did not exhibit signs of Phellinus weirii in a 1994 survey for root rots.

Comparison of the model results with literature sources indicates that dead Douglas-fir, particularly larger DBH trees, might stand longer than suggested by the default model (e.g., Harmon et al. 1986, Cline et al. 1980, and Graham 1982).

Hwc Notes

The control plots of 8 pure (>80% by basal area) western hemlock installations of EP703 were used for model parameterization. (See coastal Douglas-fir for a description of EP703). These installations are on northern Vancouver Island.  Similar fall rates are found in Graham (1982) for the Cascades in Washington State.

Pl Notes

Lodgepole pine fall rates are based on information collected from EP384 and EP385 located near Canal Flats, B.C. These experimental plots were established in 1953 and have been measured regularly at 5 year intervals. During 1980 to 1983, a mountain pine beetle infestation occurred in the stand.   Similar fall rates were found in the literature (e.g., Lyon 1977, Raphael and Morrison 1987).

Hwi Notes

Since no data were available for the fall rates of western hemlock growing in the interior, the default coefficients were set to the rates for coastal western hemlock.

Cw Notes

Western red-cedar is generally felt to be a long standing species, particularly for larger stems. We chose parameters that reflect a fall rate slower than fall rates of Douglas-fir or western hemlock for larger DBHs.

We had limited data on western red-cedar fall rates for small trees (<10cm) as a tertiary species in EP703 installations. These trees tended to fall at rates similar to Douglas-fir and western hemlock.

Sw Notes

Little information is available on white spruce. The default parameters were set to those of coastal Douglas-fir.

Ss Notes

Little information is available on Sitka Spruce. It was felt to be as long standing as Douglas-fir, and so the default parameters were set to those of coastal Douglas-fir.

Fdi Notes

Since no data were available for the fall rates of interior Douglas-fir, the default coefficients were set to the rates for coastal Douglas-fir.
