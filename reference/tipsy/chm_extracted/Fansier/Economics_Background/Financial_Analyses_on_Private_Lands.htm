Financial Analyses on Private Lands

The shift from an economic analysis of silviculture investments on Crown land to financial analysis of investments on private lands is largely a shift in who we measure the benefits and costs for. In economic analysis we attempt to measure all the benefits and costs to society, while in financial analysis we measure the benefits and costs accruing to the private landowner.

The first major difference between the assumptions of economic and financial analysis is the treatment of taxes. From the view of society as a whole, taxes are a redistribution of income among its members, and not a cost to society. Thus, we don't include taxes in an economic analysis. From a private viewpoint, however, taxes are a cost and thus are included in a financial analysis.

An example will clarify this point. Consider a society of four people, two of whom have $10 each and two who have $5 each. They agree together that the two with $10 will give $2 to the two less well-off members. Two members now have $8 and two have $7. Note that the society's total wealth was $30 both before and after the redistribution, so society as a whole is no better off either before or after the redistribution of income. A caveat to this analogy is that taxes may result in a sub-optimal allocation of resources between capital and consumption goods. This could potentially reduce society's total wealth in the future.

Property Taxes

To apply property taxes to FAN$IER, enter the annual amount you expect in the Costs Tab, under Annual Costs.  For information on how private forest land is taxed in BC, refer to the Forest Land Assessment Fact Sheet.

We assume that sales and any value-added taxes such as the G.S.T. are included in the silviculture, milling and harvest cost estimates.

The major tax modification made for private investment analysis is the inclusion of property taxes. In British Columbia we base the assessment of private forest lands on the assessed value of the bare land and on the assessed value of the trees when harvested. The B.C. Assessment Authority produces a schedule of bare land assessed values that take into account the land's accessibility, location, topography, and soil quality. See the Assessment Authority's Forest Land and Cut Timber Values Regulation. The annual property tax payable is the assessed value multiplied by the mill rate which for managed forest lands in unorganized (rural) areas is currently 0.05%. We also apply a yield tax to the value of the trees when harvested. In addition, The B.C. Assessment Authority produces a schedule of timber values for calculating the assessed value of the timber harvest to which the mill rate is applied to determine the total yield tax.

Private Discount Rate

The second major difference between the assumptions of economic and financial analysis is the discount rate chosen for the calculation of NPVs and SVs. The private discount rate reflects the cost of acquiring capital by the landowner. This is measured as the weighted average of the costs of acquiring capital from the various sources available to the landowner (i.e. loans, bond issues, equity, etc.). See Edge and Irvine (1981)¹ for more details measuring private sector opportunity costs of capital. Note that the private discount rate should also be measured in real terms, that is net of the current inflation rate.

FAN$IER lets you modify the default cost and revenue assumptions to reflect private costs and revenue assumptions. Thus, the model allows you to undertake financial analysis of private sector silviculture investments on private land on a before-income-tax basis. To see the results on an after-income-tax basis, reduce this amount by the marginal corporate or personal income tax rate.

¹ Edge, C.G. and V.B. Irvine, 1981. A practical approach to the appraisal of capital expenditures. The Society of Management Accountants of Canada, Hamilton, Ontario.
