Value of Standing Timber

If a perfectly competitive market for timber on the stump existed, i.e. many buyers and sellers, then market data could provide information to estimate the value of a stand at any given age. Where no such market exists, the common practice is to determine the stand's conversion value. To calculate the conversion value of a stand, also known as its residual value, first determine the total selling price of the end products which can be derived from the timber. Then subtract all the costs of converting the raw material (the timber) into the end products. The value remaining is the conversion value of the standing timber.

FAN$IER adopted the methods used by its predecessor, the TIPSY Economist, to calculate the stand's conversion value.

The end-product value of the standing timber is derived by following the timber through the manufacturing stages to a point where:

  • we can determine a market price for the end product;

  • the price reflects the differences in the quality of the wood contained in the timber; and

  • we can identify the cost of producing the end product.

British Columbia is divided into two “regions” for the purposes of timber appraisal; the coast and the interior. The coast region is defined as the geographical area west of Cascade Mountains (the coastal mountain range) with the exception of that portion of the Kalum Forest District lying west of the Cascade Mountains. In the coast region timber end-product values are derived from log transactions on the Vancouver Log Market (Ministry of Forests, Coast appraisal manual, 1997). Logs on this market are sold between major forest companies to other major forest companies by grade, reflecting the value of the timber contained in the logs. The costs of producing the logs can be derived from the costs of harvesting and transporting the logs to the market.

In the interior, broad log markets like the Vancouver Log Market do not exist for a number of reasons. On the coast, once the logs reach tide-water the cost of log transportation anywhere along the coast is relatively inexpensive. This means that the logs could be economically processed anywhere on the coast. In the interior, logs are transported primarily by road which is typically much more expensive. This could create a relatively small area over which logs from a cutting authority can be traded. This acts against the establishment of a broad active log market. Log markets in the interior tend to be more localized, within economic truck haul distance.

In the interior, we must follow the timber processing chain all the way to the production of lumber and residual wood chips in order to determine the appropriate end-product values for the timber (Ministry of Forests, Interior appraisal manual, 1996) ³. The appraisal system establishes the end-product values from lumber and residual wood chip production because lumber is the predominant product in the interior. In the mid-1990s, approximately 85% of the interior's round-wood is used for lumber products, while only 7% is used for veneer and 8% for pulp or other products. On the coast, the log use distribution is similar with 70% going for sawlogs, 3% for veneer, 26% for pulp logs and 1.6% exported.

We can derive the residual value of the timber at this point since:

  • market values for the lumber can be determined;

  • both lumber grades and dimensions reflect the differences in the value of the timber; and

  • the costs of lumber manufacturing can be collected to add to harvesting and transportation costs.

Regardless of whether we base the estimate of conversion value on log values or lumber values, the process is similar. First determine the gross value of the timber and then subtract the cost of conversion.

The conversion value should include a measure of the opportunity cost of the capital used to produce the end-products. In the traditional stumpage calculation, this opportunity cost is commonly referred to as an allowance for profit and risk. Such allowances were typically based on some percentage of operating costs or selling price. This was done mainly for administrative convenience rather than theoretical correctness. Instead the allowance should be based on the amount of capital employed in the conversion process. Its inclusion ensures that the producers receive a normal return, and thus the residual value is entirely attributable to the use of the land for timber production.
