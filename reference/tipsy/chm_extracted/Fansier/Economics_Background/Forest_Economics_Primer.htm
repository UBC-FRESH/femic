The following four (4) scenarios may interest you if you have not confronted formal economic analyses since Economics 101. They should help you understand the concepts underlying the economic analysis, with particular reference to pre-commercial (PCT) and commercial thinning (CT).  Refer to Net Present Value and Site Value if you are not familiar with these terms.

Final harvest costs decline because road costs are incurred at the time of commercial thinning. The revenue from the final harvest also declines because less wood is available as a result of the earlier thinning. The net present value in this example has decreased in relation to the scenario (#1) above. This implies that commercial thinning is not warranted economically. However the need to ensure a continuous flow of timber to keep mills operating, and other considerations, may justify the early entry. The fact that CT costs exceed CT revenues doesn't mean that the entry is unjustified. It may only reflect that road construction costs have shifted from the final harvest to the commercial thinning entry. All discounted costs and revenues must be considered.

The information in the last column above gives the discounted value of the current and all future rotations (infinite series). It is the net present value ($14) of the first rotation (age 0 to 60) shown in the table above, plus the discounted value of the next rotation (age 61 to 120), and so on. The site value will always exaggerate (i.e. be more positive or more negative) the net present value when all values are discounted to age 0.

The net present value ($3560) is considerably higher than in the previous scenario (#2) because the costs of planting and pre-commercial thinning are ignored. They are sunk costs from the past over which we have no control. The decision to commercially thin depends only on the evaluation of future opportunities. As described in scenario #2, the site value includes the net present value and the discounted value from all rotations.

Note: If the first rotation uses a reference age greater than zero, it is possible for the site value to be less than the net present value. Consider the case where the NPV and site value are both negative when discounted to age 0. Changing the analysis base age to 40 can increase the net present value of the current rotation considerably, as illustrated above, and make it positive. Since the discounted value of the future rotations is negative, the site value of the stand will be less than the net present value. This implies you have inherited a management regime that is not economic in the long run. But given that earlier investments are sunk, you need to know how best to manage it until there is an opportunity to harvest and start over with a profitable regime.

It appears we should harvest at age 70 to reap the maximum benefits in this highly hypothetical example. We might also look at site value because it incorporates the benefits from all future rotations, assuming the current regime is repeated in perpetuity. It is more useful than NPV because it permits the comparison of regimes with different rotation ages.
